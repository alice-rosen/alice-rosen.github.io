## Project overview

We explored how tropical forest structure changes after disturbance, and whether repeat airborne lidar can track structural recovery through time. Working across human-modified tropical landscapes in Sabah, Malaysian Borneo, we used high-resolution canopy height models to compare old-growth, recovering, restored, recently logged, salvage-logged and oil palm landscapes.

## Approach

We adapted a geometric framework originally developed for coral reef surface complexity and applied it to tropical forest canopies. The framework describes three-dimensional surfaces using height range, rugosity and fractal dimension. We tested whether these metrics could capture differences in canopy structure across disturbance classes, and whether they could detect structural change through time after logging, restoration and wind disturbance.

## Findings

The three surface-complexity metrics captured major differences among highly degraded, selectively logged and old-growth forests. They also tracked different temporal patterns of change following logging, restoration and wind disturbance. However, the analysis showed that these metrics alone cannot fully describe forest structural complexity: fractal dimension was sensitive to small changes in data inputs, and height range and rugosity were strongly linked because both depended on maximum tree height.

## Why It Matters

Forest structural complexity shapes biodiversity, ecosystem functioning and resilience, but it is difficult to summarise with a single metric. This project shows both the promise and the limits of using repeat lidar to monitor tropical forest recovery, and highlights the need for structural indicators that better represent how trees fill three-dimensional space.
